Healthcare · Evidence and oversight

AI in healthcare: a support tool, not a doctor

AI can help health teams organize information, analyze medical images and support research. The World Health Organization sees potential in these uses, while emphasizing that systems must be safe, effective and designed around people.

Where it may help

  • Flagging patterns in images for a trained professional to review
  • Supporting health research and the development of treatments
  • Helping teams summarize information, when accuracy is checked
A model's output is not a diagnosis. Qualified health professionals must remain accountable for care.

What responsible use requires

Health AI needs rigorous evaluation, privacy safeguards and oversight for bias and errors. Patients and clinicians should understand when a system is involved and what its limits are. Read the WHO guidance on ethics and governance of AI for health.

Education · Learning with support

AI in education: a tutor still needs a teacher

Generative AI can help explain a topic, suggest practice questions or give feedback on a draft. It can also produce incorrect answers and may not understand a learner's needs. UNESCO recommends a human-centred approach that protects learners and keeps educational goals in view.

Useful ways to involve AI

  1. Ask for an explanation, then compare it with class materials
  2. Generate practice prompts and work through the answers yourself
  3. Use feedback as a starting point, not as a substitute for learning

Keep learning and privacy first

Teachers and families should check age suitability, protect student information and make expectations for AI use clear. Read UNESCO's guidance for generative AI in education and research.

Work · Adoption and tasks

AI at work: adoption is not the same as job replacement

Organizations are trying AI for tasks such as drafting, summarizing, coding and information search. A 2025 Stanford AI Index report found that 78% of surveyed organizations reported using AI in 2024; 71% reported using generative AI in at least one business function. Those survey figures describe organizational use—not the share of jobs replaced or a guarantee of productivity gains.

Questions worth asking at work

  • Which task is the tool meant to help with, and how will its output be checked?
  • What company, customer or personal information may be entered?
  • Who is responsible for decisions made using the result?

How AI changes a role depends on the task, workplace and how a tool is introduced. See the Stanford AI Index 2025 report for the data and its context.

Privacy · Safer everyday use

Before you paste into an AI tool, check your privacy

AI services differ in what they collect, how long they retain prompts and whether submitted content can be used to improve a service. The exact rules depend on the product, account and settings, so check the provider's current privacy information before sharing anything sensitive.

Practical checks before sharing

  • Remove names, account numbers and other details that identify people
  • Check whether chat history, uploads or training use can be controlled
  • Follow your employer's or school's rules for confidential information
  • Use a trusted source for important decisions instead of sharing secrets to get advice

Organizations can use the NIST Privacy Framework to help identify and manage privacy risks. It is a voluntary resource, not a guarantee that any particular tool is safe.

Energy · Measuring AI's infrastructure

AI runs on data centres—and electricity

AI computation takes place on physical infrastructure that needs electricity and cooling. The International Energy Agency estimates data centres used around 415 terawatt-hours (TWh) of electricity in 2024, about 1.5% of global electricity use. This figure covers data centres overall; it is not an estimate of AI alone.

Why forecasts need context

The IEA's base case projects data-centre electricity use reaching about 945 TWh in 2030, but the report also explores alternative scenarios. Future demand depends on AI uptake, hardware and software efficiency, and energy-system constraints.

Read the IEA analysis and its scenarios.
